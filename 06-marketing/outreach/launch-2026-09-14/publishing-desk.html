LinkedIn document title
Five questions before choosing another tool
LinkedIn caption
“We need a new system” is often the first proposed solution.

It should be the sixth question.

Before choosing software, automation or AI, answer these five:

1. Trigger: what starts the work?
2. Owner: who is accountable for the next action?
3. Information: what is needed, and where should it live?
4. Exception: what happens when the normal path breaks?
5. Finish: what does done mean, and who confirms it?

If those answers are unclear, new technology can make the confusion move faster.

The smallest useful improvement may be:

- one agreed handover;
- a controlled status and next-action view;
- a simpler form;
- one stable automation;
- a lightweight internal tool; or
- no build at all.

That is the Heutrix principle: improve the workflow before adding technology.

LinkedIn caption
Illustrative operating view — not compliance or service-readiness assurance.

“Starting soon” is not a service-commencement status.

Neither is “almost ready”.

Those phrases hide the decisions an accountable person still needs to make.

A practical commencement view should show, at an appropriate level:

- the intended start date;
- the current owner;
- what is complete;
- what is still waiting;
- the next action and review date;
- any exception requiring a decision; and
- who confirms readiness under the provider’s approved process.

The workflow should not infer readiness from a green cell or a completed checklist. A named person still makes the accountable decision.

Nor does every sensitive record need to be copied into the view. Often the operating layer needs only enough controlled information to show what happens next.

Before the next commencement, finish this sentence:

Ready means ______, and ______ confirms it.

If the team completes the sentence differently, the issue is bigger than a missing reminder.

Illustrative: when no build is the useful decision
LinkedIn caption
Illustrative Heutrix application—not a client result.

The request sounds straightforward:

“Can we automate reminders for missing information?”

In this illustrative scenario, Heutrix Diagnostics identifies three problems:

- different teams disagree about what “complete” means;
- nobody owns the exception decision; and
- the data needed to trigger the reminder is not recorded consistently.

Automating now would send conflicting reminders faster.

The more useful first move is no build:

1. Agree one decision rule.
2. Name the person who approves exceptions.
3. Run a short baseline using non-sensitive operational data.
4. Reassess whether automation is still justified.

That is what good diagnosis should do: compare process clarification, a manual control, a lightweight workflow and automation—then recommend the smallest option supported by the evidence.

The value is the decision, even when the answer is “not yet”.

Save this question for the next automation request:

Is the rule stable enough to automate?

Workflow Transformation: four delivery stages
LinkedIn caption
A workflow is not transformed when the build goes live.

The team still needs to test it, understand it and take ownership.

Heutrix Workflow Transformation follows four stages:

Diagnose — understand the current workflow, its boundary and the baseline or baseline plan. This is part of implementation; it does not require a separate Diagnostics purchase.

Triage — agree the future state, responsibilities, dependencies, controls and written acceptance criteria.

Build — implement the smallest useful improvement and test it with the people who will use it. That can mean changing the process or configuring existing systems; new software is only one option.

Handover — guide and train the team, transfer agreed access and documentation, and make ownership, maintenance and limitations clear.

Mapping and design happen before implementation. Testing happens before acceptance. Handover makes the improvement usable beyond the project.

The result is one bounded non-clinical workflow implemented, tested and handed over, within a written scope.

WEEK 4 / P11 / 2026-10-14 · 08:15 AEDT

Can staff classify a proposed AI task?

LinkedIn Page · 08:15 AEDT scheduled. Upload the single PDF and paste the document title below. Reviewed copy retained; actual composer or existing scheduled preview checked 15 September 2026. scheduled; queue verified 15 September 2026; not yet published.

LinkedIn document title
AI use: approved, prohibited or review required
LinkedIn caption
Illustrative decision framework.

Your AI policy is tested when a staff member asks:

“Can I use it for this?”

A practical answer needs three clear pathways.

Approved

The task, tool, information boundaries, output use and human-review step have been assessed and recorded.

Prohibited

The use sits outside the organisation’s boundary—for example, entering personal or sensitive information into an unapproved public AI tool, or allowing AI to make an important decision without accountable human judgement.

Conditional / review required

The idea may have value, but a question about purpose, information, settings, access, retention, consequence or reviewer still needs an answer.

An unassessed use stays in review. A conditional decision must state what has to be resolved before use can proceed.

That third pathway matters. It gives staff somewhere safe to take a useful idea without assuming every new use is allowed—or forcing experimentation underground.

A short, maintained use-case register is more useful than a long policy nobody can apply.

Take one proposed administrative task and ask: which pathway is it in, who decided and when is it reviewed again? Use non-sensitive or synthetic information during assessment.

This is general operational guidance, not legal or privacy advice.
