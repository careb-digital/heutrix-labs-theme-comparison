# Week 4 — Make AI guardrails usable

## P10 — Five questions before staff use AI at work

- Publish: Week 4 Monday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider leaders and operational teams first; allied health second
- Format: text
- Status: `draft_owner_review`

### Post copy

“We have an approved AI account” does not tell a staff member whether this task is safe to do.

Before AI supports a work task, answer five questions:

1. **Use:** what exact task is approved?
2. **Information:** what may—and may not—enter the tool?
3. **Tool:** which product, plan, settings and account are approved for that use?
4. **Review:** who checks the output, and what must they test?
5. **Record:** where does the approved final version belong?

The same tool may be suitable for drafting a generic checklist from non-sensitive instructions and unsuitable for participant, worker or other personal information.

Start narrow. Test with non-sensitive or synthetic information. Keep a person accountable for the final output and the decision it supports.

AI should assist a defined workflow—not quietly become an unreviewed step inside it.

Save the five questions and test them on one proposed use case this week.

## P11 — Your AI policy fails if staff cannot classify a real task

- Publish: Week 4 Wednesday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider leaders, operational managers and staff influencers
- Format: six-page LinkedIn document
- Status: `draft_asset_and_owner_review`
- Human check: privacy/security owner reviews the examples; this is operational guidance, not legal or privacy advice.

### Post copy

Your AI policy fails at the moment a staff member asks:

“Can I use it for this?”

A practical answer needs three clear pathways.

**Approved**

The task, tool, information boundaries, output use and human-review step have been assessed and recorded.

**Prohibited**

The use sits outside the organisation’s boundary—for example, entering personal or sensitive information into an unapproved public AI tool, or allowing AI to make an important decision without accountable human judgement.

**Conditional / review required**

The idea may have value, but a question about purpose, information, settings, access, retention, consequence or reviewer still needs an answer.

An unassessed use stays in review. A conditional decision must state what has to be resolved before use can proceed.

That third pathway matters. It gives staff somewhere safe to take a useful idea without assuming every new use is allowed—or forcing experimentation underground.

A short, maintained use-case register is more useful than a long policy nobody can apply.

Take one real task and ask: which pathway is it in, who decided and when is it reviewed again?

#AIGovernance #DisabilityProviders #HumanReview

### Document brief

1. Cover: “Can I use AI for this task?”
2. Approved.
3. Prohibited.
4. Conditional / review required.
5. Record: owner, tool, information, output, reviewer, decision date.
6. Close: “A policy works when a person can make the next safe decision.”

## P12 — Removing the name does not automatically make an AI prompt safe

- Publish: Week 4 Friday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider leaders and operational teams first; allied health second
- Format: source-led text
- Status: `draft_source_and_owner_review`
- Source: [OAIC guidance on commercially available AI products](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)
- Human check: source rechecked 9 September 2026; re-open it on publication day and do not present the post as legal or privacy advice.

### Post copy

Removing the name does not automatically make an AI prompt safe.

In a small team, specialised service or unusual situation, the remaining context may still identify a person.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner recommends, as a matter of best practice, that organisations do not enter personal information—particularly sensitive information—into publicly available generative AI tools because of the significant and complex privacy risks.

That turns “Can we use AI?” into a more useful set of questions:

- What is the exact purpose?
- What information would enter the tool?
- Is the tool, account and setting approved for that use?
- Who can access the input and output?
- How could the provider store, retain or reuse the information?
- Who checks accuracy and remains accountable?

While an idea is being assessed, use non-sensitive or synthetic information and obtain qualified advice where required.

Read the OAIC guidance: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products
