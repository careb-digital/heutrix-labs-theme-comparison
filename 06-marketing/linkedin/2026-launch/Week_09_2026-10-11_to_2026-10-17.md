# Week 9 — AI use cases, review and trust

## P25 — Low-risk, higher-risk and unsuitable AI uses

- Publish: Week 9 Monday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider decision-makers first; allied-health decision-makers second
- Format: source-led text
- Status: `draft_source_and_owner_review`
- Source: [OAIC guidance on commercially available AI products](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)
- Human check: examples are screening prompts, not legal classifications. OAIC source rechecked 9 September 2026; re-open it before publication.

### Post copy

AI risk belongs to the use case, not just the tool name.

A practical screening conversation might begin like this.

**Potentially lower-risk candidate**

Drafting a generic meeting agenda from non-sensitive instructions, using an approved tool, with a person reviewing the result.

**Needs careful review**

Summarising internal material, handling any personal information, generating communication that will go to a patient or participant, or producing content used in an important business decision.

**Outside Heutrix's recommended boundaries**

Putting identifiable or sensitive information into an unapproved public AI tool, or allowing AI to make a clinical, safety, employment, financial, legal or other important decision without accountable human judgement.

These are not universal legal categories. The real assessment needs the purpose, information, provider, settings, access, retention, consequences and human control.

Start narrow. Record the decision. Reassess when the tool or use changes.

## P26 — “Human review” needs a named person and a test

- Publish: Week 9 Wednesday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider decision-makers first; allied-health decision-makers second
- Format: text
- Status: `draft_owner_review`

### Post copy

“A human will review it” is not a control until the workflow answers four questions.

**Who?**

Name the role accountable for the review.

**What?**

Define what they check: accuracy, completeness, tone, permissions, source support, privacy, professional judgement or another relevant criterion.

**When?**

Place the review before the output is sent, recorded, relied on or used for a decision.

**What if it is wrong?**

Make it easy to reject, correct, escalate and record a material issue.

A reviewer who is rushed, lacks context or assumes the AI is usually right may only rubber-stamp the output.

Good human review is designed into the workflow. It is not a disclaimer added at the end.

For one approved AI use case, write the reviewer and their test in one sentence.

## P27 — What Australians expect before AI affects them

- Publish: Week 9 Friday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider decision-makers first; allied-health decision-makers second
- Format: data graphic plus text
- Status: `draft_source_and_owner_review`
- Source: [OAIC Australian Community Attitudes to Privacy Survey 2026](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0023/264362/ACAPS-2026-Report.PDF), pages 59–60.
- Human check: report pages 59–60 and sample methodology rechecked 9 September 2026; confirm again before publication. Preserve the context that the question concerned AI decisions that might affect the respondent.

### Post copy

Trust in AI is operational.

In the OAIC's nationally representative Australian Community Attitudes to Privacy Survey 2026:

- 81% considered a right to human review essential before an organisation uses AI to make a decision that might affect them.
- 79% wanted to be told that AI was being used.
- 80% wanted personal information not to be retained by a third-party AI provider beyond what is necessary.

The survey included 1,504 Australian adults.

For a disability support provider—and for an allied health practice facing the same issue—those expectations should change the workflow, not just the privacy statement.

Where AI is being considered, make the use visible. Define the information boundary. Check provider handling. Name the accountable reviewer. Give people a meaningful path to question or correct an output where appropriate.

Trust is easier to preserve when those controls exist before deployment.

Source: OAIC, Australian Community Attitudes to Privacy Survey 2026: https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0023/264362/ACAPS-2026-Report.PDF
