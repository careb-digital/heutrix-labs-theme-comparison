## P01 — When important work disappears between people and systems

- Publish: activation Monday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: Australian disability support providers first; allied health practices second

- Status: `draft_asset_and_owner_review`

### Post copy

Important work rarely disappears all at once.

It disappears one handover at a time.

An intake waits in an inbox. A reporting input sits with the wrong owner. A required document is found missing only when someone needs it. A manager asks for an update, and the team rebuilds the story from messages, spreadsheets and memory.

Capable people can work hard inside a workflow that still makes the next action difficult to see.

That is the problem Heutrix exists to help solve.

Heutrix grew from first-hand experience running a disability and allied health services organisation.

We help Australian disability support providers improve recurring non-clinical workflows. We also work with allied health practices facing the same operating pattern.

Our approach is simple:

- understand how the work happens now;
- clarify ownership, status, handovers and exceptions;
- choose the smallest useful improvement; and
- use technology only where it supports a defined result.

The answer may be Heutrix Diagnostics, Heutrix Workflow Transformation, Heutrix AI Guardrails—or an honest recommendation not to proceed.

Workflow first. Technology second.

Follow Heutrix for practical field notes on intake, reporting preparation, evidence and documents, handovers and responsible workplace AI.

Where does status become guesswork in your organisation? Keep the answer high level—no participant, worker, client or other sensitive information.

## P02 — Before buying another tool, answer five workflow questions

### Post copy

“We need a new system” is often the first proposed solution.

It should be the sixth question.

Before choosing software, automation or AI, answer these five:

1. **Trigger:** what starts the work?
2. **Owner:** who is accountable for the next action?
3. **Information:** what is needed, and where should it live?
4. **Exception:** what happens when the normal path breaks?
5. **Finish:** what does done mean, and who confirms it?

If those answers are unclear, new technology can make the confusion move faster.

The smallest useful improvement may be:

- one agreed handover;
- a controlled status and next-action view;
- a simpler form;
- one stable automation;
- a lightweight internal tool; or
- no build at all.

That is the Heutrix principle: improve the workflow before adding technology.

If several workflows are competing for attention, use the free Workflow Bottleneck Scorecard to assess one at a time. The interactive tool suggests a next step from the friction, evidence and readiness you report. The accompanying PDF guide and workbook help you compare workflows. No email address is required.

A self-assessment is a starting point, not a verified business case.

## P03 — Seven signs a workflow depends on memory

- Publish: activation Friday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider decision-makers and operational influencers
- Format: text
- Status: `draft_owner_review`

### Post copy

Your workflow may be a shared memory disguised as a system.

Seven signs:

1. A manager has to ask several people for one update.
2. Staff keep side spreadsheets to make the main process usable.
3. The same information is copied into more than one place.
4. A waiting item is discovered only when someone chases it.
5. Work slows down when one key person is away.
6. “Done” means something different across the team.
7. Reporting begins with a manual clean-up before anyone trusts it.

None of these proves you need a new platform.

They do suggest one repeated workflow deserves a closer look.

Start small: choose one item that entered the process this week. Can the right person see its owner, status, next action, review date and finish without asking for the backstory?

If not, that is a useful place to begin.

Which sign would you inspect first? Keep examples general and leave participant, worker and confidential information out of the comments.
