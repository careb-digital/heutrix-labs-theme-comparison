# Week 5 — How Heutrix diagnoses a workflow

## P13 — What happens in Heutrix Diagnostics

- Publish: Week 5 Monday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider decision-makers first; allied-health decision-makers second
- Format: process diagram plus text
- Status: `draft_asset_and_owner_review`
- Human check: delivery and commercial owners must confirm the current scope against the [8 September working architecture](../../../02-market-and-offers/offers/offer-strategy-2026-09/06-integrated-offer-architecture.md). Its new readout, format and completion requirements remain proposed; do not advertise them as standard inclusions. Do not add a public price or starting range; model approval controls private quoting.

### Post copy

Heutrix Diagnostics should create a decision—not a pile of process diagrams.

The Heutrix approach starts with an agreed workflow or tightly related problem area:

1. Define the problem and where the workflow starts and ends.
2. Map the current state, ownership, information, handovers and exceptions.
3. Agree a baseline or a practical baseline plan.
4. Rank bottlenecks, risks and dependencies.
5. Compare feasible options, including process-only and no-build choices.
6. Recommend a future state, success measures and written no-go conditions.
7. Provide a decision and next-action brief, with an implementation scope only where justified.

The result remains useful if the organisation implements the recommendation internally, chooses another provider or stops.

Heutrix Diagnostics is not implementation, legal or clinical advice, compliance assurance or an open-ended review of every system and document.

The investigation coverage and outputs are agreed in writing. Where evidence is incomplete, the recommendation should say what remains unknown and what would resolve it.

Talk to Heutrix with a high-level description of the workflow—without sensitive information.

### Asset brief

Create a seven-step vertical process. Keep the text mobile-readable. The final box should say “A decision-ready brief”, not “technology build”.

## P14 — Current state and future state are not just boxes

- Publish: Week 5 Wednesday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider decision-makers first; allied-health decision-makers second
- Format: LinkedIn document
- Status: `draft_asset_and_owner_review`

### Post copy

A workflow map is useful only when it explains how the work behaves.

A current-state map should show more than task boxes. It should expose:

- who owns each action
- what information is needed
- where the information lives
- which decisions change the path
- what happens when something is missing
- where work waits
- how the process is closed

The future state should then make deliberate choices.

Which step is removed?

Which handover becomes clearer?

Which status becomes visible?

Which reminder is appropriate?

Which decision must remain human?

Which information should not be copied into another system?

Without those choices, a “future-state map” can be the same process with new colours and an automation icon.

The value is not in drawing the boxes. It is in deciding how the work should operate.

#ProcessMapping #WorkflowDesign #BusinessAnalysis

### Document brief

Use six pages: cover; ownership; information; decisions and exceptions; waiting and closure; future-state design questions. Avoid a dense swimlane screenshot.

## P15 — Do not automate this yet

- Publish: Week 5 Friday, 8:15 am Australia/Sydney
- Audience: disability-provider decision-makers first; allied-health decision-makers second
- Format: text
- Status: `draft_owner_review`

### Post copy

Sometimes the most useful automation advice is: do not automate this yet.

Pause when:

- different people cannot agree on the current process
- the same status means different things to different teams
- important exceptions are handled informally
- nobody owns the final outcome
- the input data is incomplete or unreliable
- the proposed automation would use sensitive information without approved controls
- the process itself may no longer be necessary

Automation can reduce repeated manual work.

It can also scale ambiguity, create harder-to-find errors and make a poor process more difficult to change.

First agree on the trigger, owner, information, exceptions and definition of done.

Then decide whether automation is the smallest useful improvement.

The goal is not to automate more. It is to make the workflow more reliable.
